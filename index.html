Job Market Paper

How Foreign Investment Shapes Conflict: Evidence from Myanmar
Abstract
How does foreign investment shape violence in fragile states? I study this question in Myanmar, where foreign capital expanded during a decade marked by political transition, armed conflict, and the 2021 military coup. I combine a township-year panel for 2015–2024 with sectoral foreign direct investment (FDI) approvals and pre-period employment shares to construct local shift-share measures of exposure to national FDI movements. I distinguish resource from non-resource investment and disaggregate conflict fatalities by the identities of perpetrators and victims. The aggregate relationship between FDI exposure and conflict is weak, but this masks pronounced sectoral differences. Greater resource-sector exposure is associated with less state–nonstate battlefield violence but more violence by non-state actors against civilians. By contrast, greater non-resource exposure is associated with lower fatalities overall, with the largest reductions in nonstate violence against civilians. These findings suggest that the consequences of foreign investment for conflict depend not simply on how much investment arrives, but on the sectors it enters and the forms of violence it reshapes.

Working Papers

Vertical Integration in Uncompetitive Markets:
Evidence from the acquisition of North Star Air by the North West Company
with Nicholas Li
Abstract
This paper studies the 2017 acquisition of North Star Air (NSA), a regional cargo and passenger carrier, by the North West Company (NWC), a major retailer serving remote northern Canadian communities. We assemble a quarterly panel of 125 communities from 2013 to 2022 and examine changes in Revised Northern Food Basket prices and subsidized shipment quantities. In pooled difference-in-differences specifications, NSAroute exposure is associated with approximately 3 percent lower basket prices and 7 percent higher shipment weight. The estimates are concentrated on routes served before the acquisition and routes added during the later expansion; effects for the initial expansion are small and imprecise. For newly served routes, the price decline is concentrated in communities with local retail competition and is largely offset in NWC-monopoly communities. Retailer-specific data show higher NWC shipments in pooled and late-route markets, but no overall increase in NWC’s shipment share or clean evidence of rival displacement. We construct an asymmetric two-carrier, two-retailer model to organize our conceptual framework in helping us uncover cases for reduction in double marginalization and integration synergies.
The Costs of Extreme Remoteness
with Nicholas Li
Abstract
We examine how well available measures of remoteness capture differences in food costs, freight costs, and other welfare-relevant outcomes across remote communities in Canada. Our analysis combines the Statistics Canada Remoteness Index with several independent measures of spatial costs. These include the Federal Isolated Posts Allowance Index and Living Cost Differential, territorial community price indexes, Nutrition North Canada Revised Northern Food Basket prices, and, where possible, commercial air freight rates and online retailer shipping costs. We also examine how community size and specific transportation characteristics, including road, rail, fly-in, and seasonal surface access, interact with remoteness. Further, we examine whether the organization and scale of retail and distribution systems affect costs independently of community population. These comparisons allow us to assess whether communities that appear similarly remote according to a gravity-based measure actually face similar costs, and whether alternative spatial price and transportation measures provide additional information about the economically relevant dimensions of remoteness.

Strengthening Food Access in Nunavut: Lessons from the Inuit Child First Initiative funded Grocery Supports and the Hamlet Food Voucher Program (2026)
with Sinha, V., Li, N., Hoang, N., Reddy, D., Darkey, J., Mitchell, K., Gilbert, S.Z., Wheatley, D., Govindapillai, S., Roher, S., Khan, M. Galloway, T.
Summary
In this report, we document the state of food insecurity and the impact of ICFI-funded food supports for families in Nunavut. Existing research demonstrates that nearly 8 out of 10 Inuit children were food insecure before the start of the HFV, that food insecurity is linked to serious physical and psychological harm and that food insecurity in Nunavut is driven by deep structural inequities. Our research shows that Inuit families are drawing on their individual and collective strengths, and a sophisticated range of budget-stretching strategies, to try to ensure access to food, with dignity, for everyone in the community
Measurement and analysis of food price levels and inflation in Nunavut, 2017-2025 (2026)
with Li, N., Morrison, M., Sinha, V., Wheatley, D.
Summary
The aim of this report is to provide the most comprehensive analysis of food prices in Nunavut to date. We analyzed multiple sources of Nunavut food price data to provide a comprehensive assessment of current price levels (i.e. how the price of a product or basket of products compares to Canadian averages) and recent inflation (i.e. how much prices have increased in Nunavut compared to increases in Canadian average prices).
Preliminary findings from an evaluation of the Hamlet Food Voucher Program
in Qikiqtaaluk, Nunavut: Technical report (2025)
with Galloway, T., Govindapillai, S., Hoang, N, Khan, M.N., Lavoie, J.G., Li, N., Mitchell, K., Penney, J., Reddy, D., Wheatley, D., Sinha, V.)
Summary
In this report, we present preliminary findings from a multidisciplinary, mixed methods study that started as an evaluation of individual requests for grocery supports in Qikiqtaaluk and evolved as the Hamlet Food Voucher programs came into place and were then discontinued. We draw on key informant interviews with 21 people living/working in seven hamlets or working at the regional level (Qikiqtaaluk or Nunavut). we draw on existing literature to briefly describe food insecurity in the Qikiqtaaluk region of Nunavut as a long-term, systemic problem. It is grounded in colonialism and compounded by a gap between the support available through government benefits (like income assistance and child benefits) and the cost of living in Nunavut
